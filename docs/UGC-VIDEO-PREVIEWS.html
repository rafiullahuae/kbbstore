The first new thing

The teaser loop — and how the 2–3 seconds is produced

Every tile on this page now plays a muted 2–3 second loop the moment it is more than 60% on screen, and stops the moment it is not. The loop is the easy half. The decisions underneath it are below, each one with the number that decided it.

The one that costs money: where the 2–3 seconds comes from.

There are two ways to show a three-second loop and they look identical on screen. One of them costs twelve times as much. Both were built and both were measured in Chromium against a real HTTP server, counting the bytes the browser actually asked for — a rail of eight tiles, swiped end to end.

How the loop is madeSecond asset?Video bytes, rail of 8Per tile

C — the same clip with a media fragment #t=0,2.5 none12,786,600 B  (12.19 MB)1.52 MB
B — a separate 2.5 s teaser clip, looped
2.5 s, 360×640, ~400 kbps		 one per video1,055,160 B  (1.01 MB)129 KB
Seeking back to zero does not save one byte, and the media fragment does not either.

A tells the element to play only the first 2.5 seconds; the browser fetched all 1,598,325 bytes of every file anyway — 8 × the whole clip, to the byte. C adds #t=0,2.5, which is the obvious clever answer, and the counter reads exactly the same number. A media fragment tells the player where to start, not the network what to fetch.

The recommendation is B, a separate teaser clip, and the saving is 12.1×. It is not only shorter — it is a different rendition for a different box. A rail tile is 158 CSS px wide (206 on a desktop), so 316–412 device pixels, and 360×640 at 400 kbps is the right encode for it. The full clip has to stay 720×1280 because the opened player is full screen. A can never have that saving, because it has only one file.

What the shop has to do to produce it. One extra derivative per video, cut from the file that was already uploaded: -ss 0 -t 2.5 -vf scale=360:640 -b:v 400k, no audio. It is the same transcode step §3.4 of the plan already says the upload needs, with one more output — so it costs the pipeline nothing new, and it costs the owner nothing at all: he is never asked for a second video. If the transcode cannot run (no ffmpeg on that host — still unanswered, and it is question 4 in the plan), the honest fallback is not to fall back to A. It is to ship the rail with poster frames only until the teaser exists, which is exactly what this page does under Save-Data, and costs 22 KB a tile instead of 1.52 MB.

The other decisionsWhat was doneWhy, and what it would cost to get wrong
Autoplay at all muted, playsinline, webkit-playsinline, and play()'s promise is caught. Every browser refuses an unmuted autoplay and iOS Safari additionally refuses one without playsinline — it takes the video full screen instead, which is worse than nothing. The rejected promise is caught because an autoplay refusal is a normal outcome, not an error: uncaught it is an unhandled rejection in the console on every tile.
Only what is on screen plays One IntersectionObserver at threshold 0.6, plus a hard cap of 4 at once. Eight clips decoding at once is the real cost on a mid-range phone. Measured on this page at 390px, the viewport itself never lets more than 2 tiles over the threshold, so the cap never binds on a phone — it is there for the 12-tile wall on a desktop, where it does. A tile that leaves does not just pause: it drops its src and calls load(), which is what hands the decoder back. No scroll handler and no getBoundingClientRect anywhere — this project forbids the element-measuring APIs by name in two tests.
prefers-reduced-motion: reduce Nothing autoplays. The poster frame stands, and the strip under the toolbar says so. An accessibility requirement, not a nicety: motion that starts without being asked for is exactly what the query exists to stop. Read live through matchMedia, so changing the OS setting with the page open takes effect without a reload.
Save-Data Honoured where it is sent: navigator.connection.saveData === true and nothing plays. Absent means no. Safari and Firefox do not implement navigator.connection at all, so the property is undefined on a large share of the traffic — treating unknown as "save data" would silently turn the feature off for most of the people who can afford it. The strict === true is the whole of the decision.
The first frame A real poster image on every tile, always, under the video. A tile that has not loaded, will not play, or has been told not to play still looks deliberate instead of black. The poster is also what holds the box: aspect-ratio:9/16 plus a poster means zero layout shift before any video exists.
What this page fakes, and what a real deployment would carry.

The six clips here are ~2.46 second VP8 files inlined as data URIs, so they are teasers and they loop natively — but nothing is fetched over a network, so the byte table above was measured in a separate harness with real HTTP, not in this page. A real deployment stores two files per video under /uploads/ugc/: the full clip (720×1280 H.264 MP4, ~1.5 MB for 15 s) and the teaser (360×640, 2.5 s, ~130 KB), plus a ~22 KB WebP poster.

The second new thing

The rating bar, on the video

A small bar on the tile carrying the score, five stars and the review count. It is the real pair — products.rating and products.review_count, the same two columns the shop cards, ?sort=rating and the schema.org aggregateRating already read. Nothing here is a display-only number.

DecisionWhat it doesWhy
A product with no reviews No bar at all. Look at the third tile in every rail — The SPF I actually reapply, which sells one product with no reviews yet. An empty five-star row reads as rated badly, and a zero reads as rated zero. A "New" chip in its place is a claim the data does not support — a product can be three years old and simply unreviewed. The shop already draws this line: the card badge is ! $p->review_count and the migration that retired the demo ratings says those products "end at 0/0 and their card shows the New badge instead of a star row". The bar is absolutely positioned, so its absence leaves no hole to fill.
It must not move the tile The bar lives inside .over, which is inset:auto 0 0 0. Absolute, so it adds exactly 0px to the tile and the overlay grows upward into the scrim that was already there. Every rail height printed on this page is unchanged from round two, and document.documentElement.scrollWidth is still 390 and 1280.
Reading it against a bright frame A 78% scrim, white score text, amber ★ at #FFC53D, and the empty stars at 45% white. Contrast over video is a correctness problem: a 4.8 nobody can read is not shown. 78% is the lowest alpha at which all three of score, filled star and empty star clear their thresholds against a pure white frame. The strip below shows the same bar over the brightest and darkest frames in this library and over white and black, with the ratios read off the rendered pixels.
Arabic and RTL Flex row for the stars, <bdi> around the score and the count, and Western digits. A flex row follows direction, so the filled stars sit at the inline start in both directions with no [dir] rule and no transform to flip. (1,284) without a <bdi> paints its brackets mirrored inside an Arabic run. The digits are Western because the price two lines below them is: App\Support\Money prints AED 1,199 in Western digits on the Arabic storefront and App\Support\Bidi isolates it there, so Arabic-Indic numerals in the rating and Western in the price would be two numbering systems in one tile.
Where it is not.

The bar is on the video and nowhere else. It is deliberately not added to the product card under the tile or to the cards inside the player: both of those are in the layout, so a line of stars there is a line of extra height in a box that is already tight — the rail card was cut back to name, price and Add in round two for exactly that reason.

The same bar, over four frames

The ratios under each one are read off the rendered PNG, not computed from the CSS: the bar is screenshotted at 4×, the scrim is taken as the modal colour of the image, the filled star as the pixel furthest into red-minus-blue and the empty star as the brightest neutral pixel after it, and WCAG 2.1 relative luminance is computed from those. Thresholds are 4.5:1 for the score, which is text, and 3:1 for the stars, which are not. The product is the 4.4 one, deliberately — five filled stars would hide the empty star, and the empty star is the lowest-contrast thing in the bar.

Part one

The rail, and six variants of it

Every one is the same structure: portrait videos in a row that scrolls sideways, with snap points. What changes between them is how the tagged products appear and where the card sits. The measured height of each rail on a 390px phone is printed under it, because the benchmark's rail was tall and pushed the rest of the page down.

All seven now carry both new things — the 2–3 second muted loop and the rating bar — so the loop is not a reason to prefer one rail over another. The heights are unchanged from round two, because the bar is absolutely positioned and adds no height at all.

Variant 5 · the performance fork, recast

R5 — The same loop, produced from the full clip

R5 used to be "the one that autoplays". That decision has been overtaken: you asked for the loop everywhere, so every rail on this page autoplays now and R5's old fork no longer exists. What is left of it is the fork that still costs money — where the 2–3 seconds comes from. R5's tiles play the whole clip and seek back to zero; every other rail loops a short teaser file. Look at them side by side: you cannot tell.

Changed from R0: the loop is cut out of the full clip at playback instead of being a separate file. Costs: 12.19 MB against 1.01 MB for a rail of eight swiped end to end — measured over real HTTP, not estimated, and the table at the top of this page has the run. Nothing is saved by seeking, and nothing is saved by #t=0,2.5 either: the browser fetches every byte of every file in both cases. In exchange R5 needs no second asset and no transcode step — which is the only argument for it, and it is not enough.
